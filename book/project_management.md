# Project Management

Project management is the process of turning an idea into a finished result. It helps a group agree on **what needs to be done, who will do it, when it must be completed, and how everyone will decide whether the result is good enough** through a reproducible, auditible assured process.


```{example}
  You are a team of 3 people trying to organise a party. There are several tasks that needs to be accomplished: buying snacks, decorate, design cocktails, tidy up, etc.

``` 


## 1. Agile

**Agile** is an approach in which a team delivers work in small stages, reviews progress regularly, and adjusts the plan when learning something new. It originated in software development but can be useful for many projects.
Instead of trying to plan every detail at the beginning, an Agile team maintains a prioritised list of work called a **backlog**. The team selects a manageable amount of work, completes it, gathers feedback, and then decides what to do next.

Common Agile concepts include:

- **Backlog:** The complete prioritised list of work.
- **Sprint:** A short, fixed working period, commonly one to three weeks.
- **Daily stand-up:** A brief meeting about progress, planned work, and blockers.
- **Sprint planning:** The team chooses what it can realistically complete next.
- **Review:** team members check the completed work and provide feedback.
- **Retrospective:** The team discusses what worked, what did not, and what to improve.


```{example}
For the party example, the first sprint could involve creating and sending invitations. At the end of this sprint, the review would involve checking if anyone was missed, how the process worked, etc. Having sent the invitations ( and hopefully receiving replies) also allows to plan more accurately for the next sprint which could involve making decisions around food, drink and entertainment.

``` 

 Agile allows to quinkly identify issues with workload, timings, budget and other considerations by being incremental and iterative focusing on collaboration and flexibility with each new sprint. 

While there are several project management tools, Jira is the most used in software development.


## 2. Project board and Issue tracker

Jira consists of a **project board** which shows the status of each piece of work identified through "cards" named **issues**. Depending on the project, an issue might represent an epic, story, task, sub-task, or bug. For a beginner, it is enough to start with tasks and sub-tasks. 

A simple board can use these columns to record the issues stauses:

1. **Backlog:** Ideas and future work not yet ready to begin.
2. **In Progress:** Work currently being completed.
3. **In Review:** Work waiting for checking or approval.
4. **Blocked:** Work that cannot continue because something is missing.
5. **Done:** Work that meets the agreed Definition of Done.

Each task should include:

- A short, specific title
- A description of the required outcome
- One accountable owner
- Priority and target date
- Dependencies and related tasks
- Comments recording important decisions

Large tasks should be split into smaller tasks that can usually be completed within a few working days. 

```{example}
“Organise food” is too broad because it includes several decisions and actions and may take more than the number of weeks in a sprint so it could be divided into:

1. Collect attendees dietary and allergy requirements.
2. Confirm budget
3. Confirm number of attendees
4. Compare price, quality of snacks and allergen controls
5. Buy snacks
6. Arrange snacks the day of

``` 

The project manager should review the board regularly. A weekly review may be enough for a small project, while a busy delivery team might update it daily. Tasks should move across the board as their status changes. If a task is blocked, the reason and the person responsible for resolving it should be recorded.

## 3. Definition of Done and Acceptance criteria

The **Definition of Done**, often shortened to **DoD**, is the team's shared checklist for deciding whether work is genuinely complete. It prevents someone from marking a task “Done” when it is only partly finished.

While the Definition of Done applies to every task, the **acceptance criteria** describes the specific conditions necessary for one specific task.


```{example}

In our party example “the food choices takes into account dietary requirements ” is an acceptance criterion for the "choosing food" task. 


While a shared DoD for all the tasks might include:

- acceptance criteria have been met.
- another party co-host has confirmed the task has been completed.
- receipts are attached or linked (if relevant).
- Costs are recorded against the budget ( if relevant).
- every co-host has been informed of the task and its completion
- Follow-up work has either been completed or created as a separate task.

``` 


## 4. Well defined Roles and Responsibilities

Clear roles prevent duplicated work, missed decisions, and confusion about authority. One person may hold several roles on a small project, but the responsibilities should still be explicit.

- **Project Manager:** Maintains the plan and project board, coordinates people, tracks deadlines, costs, risks, and dependencies, and reports progress.
- **Product Owner:** Represents the desired outcome, prioritises the backlog, clarifies requirements, and accepts completed work. 
- **Team Members:** Complete assigned tasks, update their status, produce evidence, and raise blockers early. 



## 5. Project Review Criteria ( Advanced )

Project managers and stakeholders may need to continually  assess the success and effectiveness of a project. These criteria typically include:

- **Objectives and Scope**: Ensuring that the project's goals were clearly defined and achieved.
- **Timeline and Milestones**: Evaluating adherence to schedules and the completion of critical milestones.
-**Resource Allocation**: Analyzing the efficiency of resource utilization, including human capital and budget.
-**Risk Management**: Reviewing the effectiveness of risk identification, mitigation, and response strategies.
-**Stakeholder Engagement**: Assessing the level of communication and collaboration with all project stakeholders.
-**Quality Assurance**: Examining the processes in place to ensure deliverables meet quality standards.

These criteria help project managers and stakeholders make informed decisions, identify areas for improvement, and ensure alignment with organizational goals. They also serve as a basis for future planning and execution of projects. 

Establishing **workflows** may help with assessing these criteria. A workflow is a structured process that outlines the sequence of tasks, responsibilities, and approvals needed to keep projects organized and teams aligned. It typically includes stages such as requirement gathering, planning, design, development, testing, deployment, and continuous monitoring.

 By following a standardized workflow, businesses can improve collaboration, maintain software quality, reduce delays, and deliver projects more efficiently. A structured workflow not only provides operational efficiency but it enables organizations to build better software while reducing unnecessary costs and delays.
